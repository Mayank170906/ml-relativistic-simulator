Learning Special-Relativistic Dynamics from a Custom Physics Simulator

This report documents a study of whether general-purpose regression models — from linear regression to gradient-boosted trees to small neural networks — can recover exact special-relativistic dynamics from synthetic data, and how their accuracy behaves under distribution shift toward near-light-speed regimes. It covers the physics engine and dataset design, the modeling methodology, full terminology and notation, a summary of results, a discussion of the reasoning behind key design decisions, and the project's conclusions and open questions.

Section 1

Abstract

Special-relativistic mechanics governs the motion of massive particles at any speed below c, and departs sharply from Newtonian mechanics as speed approaches the speed of light. This study asks whether standard, physics-agnostic regression models can recover eight relativistic quantities — position, velocity, β, γ, momentum, kinetic energy, total energy, and proper time — from a one-dimensional constant-force trajectory, using only five input parameters (mass, force, initial velocity, initial β, and elapsed time) and no explicit encoding of relativistic structure.

Ground truth was generated by an exact, closed-form physics engine built for this purpose and published as an independent package, relativistic-simulator. Nine models — seven scikit-learn regressors and two PyTorch multilayer perceptrons — were trained on a 1,000,000-row dataset and evaluated on both a held-out normal test split and an independently generated extreme test set weighted toward near-light-speed cases. Two dataset iterations were run: one restricting training to β ≤ 0.8 (Iteration 1) and one spanning the full range up to β = 0.999999 (Iteration 2).

The central finding is that the composition of the training distribution affects accuracy far more than model choice: Iteration-1 models produced catastrophic failures under extrapolation (R² as low as −1.7×10⁹), while Iteration-2 models, trained on the same target set but a wider distribution, achieved R² above 0.95 for several targets with no comparable degradation on the same extreme test file. Momentum, kinetic energy, and total energy remained comparatively difficult to learn even in-distribution, and gamma showed the widest spread in accuracy across model families. Full results are on the Results page; this report provides the reasoning, terminology, and conclusions behind them.

Section 2

Introduction

Ordinary Newtonian mechanics (F = ma) is an excellent approximation at everyday speeds but becomes inaccurate as an object's speed approaches c, where relativistic effects — time dilation, mass-energy scaling, and the impossibility of reaching or exceeding light-speed — dominate. This project investigates a machine-learning question rather than a physics one: given accurate synthetic examples of relativistic motion, can general-purpose regression models learn the underlying input–output mapping well enough to serve as a surrogate for the exact physics, and how does that accuracy hold up when the model is queried outside the range of speeds it was trained on?

This question matters for two reasons. First, it is a controlled test of a common concern in applied machine learning: a model's apparent accuracy on held-out data can be an artifact of how well that data resembles the training distribution, rather than evidence that the model has learned the generating process. Second, the physical quantities involved span smooth, bounded functions (proper time), unbounded divergent functions (gamma), and quantities with heavy-tailed dynamic range (momentum, energy) — giving a natural test bed for how function shape interacts with learnability, independent of any physics-specific inductive bias in the models used.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Methodology describes the physics engine, dataset generation, and modeling pipeline, including the chronological experimental history; Terminology & Notation defines every physical and machine-learning term used across this site; Results Summary gives the headline findings, with full detail on the Results page; Discussion addresses the reasoning behind specific design decisions; and Conclusions & Future Work states what is confirmed, what is confounded, what remains hypothetical, and what a follow-up study should do next.

Section 7

Conclusions & Future Work

Every claim below traces to a specific notebook, result file, or model artifact in the repository. Findings are labeled by confidence level: CONFIRMED (directly supported by a result, no caveats), CONFOUNDED (the supporting experiment had a design limitation that weakens the conclusion), HYPOTHESIS (a plausible but untested explanation), and OBSERVATION (a verified fact, not itself a value judgment).

Failure analysis

CONFIRMED

Extrapolation beyond the training β range fails catastrophically

Iteration-1 models (trained only on β≤0.8) reached R² as low as −1.7×10⁹ on some targets when evaluated on β up to 0.999999. Polynomial regression (degrees 3–4) and even gradient-boosted trees produced negative R² on position, gamma, momentum, and both energy targets. Only velocity/beta, which stay bounded even at extreme β, degraded gracefully (R²≈0.45–0.50). Source: data/extreme_test_results.csv, notebooks-oldData/evaluation.ipynb

CONFIRMED

Gamma and kinetic energy are the two hardest targets in-distribution

Even with training data spanning the full β range (Iteration 2), gamma's R² ranges from 0.01 (Linear Regression) to 0.98 (Hist Gradient Boosting) — the widest spread of any target — and kinetic energy's best achievable R² across all 7 sklearn models tops out at 0.781, the lowest ceiling of any target. Source: notebooks/physics_data_sklearn_results.csv

CONFOUNDED

The polynomial-feature MLP underperforms the plain MLP — but not on equal footing

Mean R² dropped from 0.715 (plain MLP) to 0.340 (polynomial-feature MLP) on the normal test. However, the polynomial run used a batch size of 524,288 versus 4,096 for the plain MLP — far fewer gradient updates per epoch — and its validation loss was still descending at epoch 50, unlike the plain MLP which had plateaued. This experiment cannot cleanly isolate whether explicit polynomial features hurt, help, or are neutral. Source: notebooks/PolynomialNeuralNetwork.ipynb, notebooks/neuralnetwork.ipynb

HYPOTHESIS

Log-uniform mass/force sampling may unevenly weight the loss for unbounded targets

Momentum and energy span many orders of magnitude under the project's log-uniform mass (10⁰–10⁶) and force (10⁰–10¹⁰) sampling. This plausibly explains why these targets underperform bounded ones (velocity, beta, proper time) even though momentum is linear in its inputs by construction — but this project did not run a controlled ablation (e.g. training on log-target space) to confirm it.

Observations

OBSERVATION

"Extreme" test sets are not automatically out-of-distribution

The same physical file, relativistic_extreme_test_10k.npy, is a genuine extrapolation test against Iteration-1 training (β≤0.8) but not against Iteration-2 training (β up to 0.999999 already included). The plain MLP's extreme-test R² (0.748) actually exceeds its normal-test R² (0.715), which only makes sense once each test set's relationship to training is examined directly — a reminder that out-of-distribution status must be verified against the specific training distribution, not inferred from a file name.

OBSERVATION

Velocity and beta are redundant learning targets

Because β=v/c is an exact linear rescaling, every sklearn model produces numerically identical R² for velocity and beta (verified row-by-row in physics_data_sklearn_results.csv). The 8-target multi-output setup is effectively learning 7 independent quantities, not 8.

OBSERVATION

Simple tree models sometimes outperform boosted ensembles on specific targets

A plain Decision Tree (max_depth=25) achieves the best R² of any sklearn model on both momentum (0.803) and kinetic energy (0.781) — outperforming both Random Forest and Extra Trees, and trailing the boosted models only marginally elsewhere. Model ranking is target-dependent rather than universal.

OBSERVATION

The physics engine's own validation is exact, not approximate

The dataset's ground truth passed the engine's independent validation checks (max energy-momentum invariant error 3.2×10⁻¹⁵, zero velocity-bound violations) — meaning every error reported throughout this site is attributable to the learned models, not to label noise in the simulator.

Future work

  • Re-run the polynomial-feature MLP with a batch size matched to the plain MLP (4,096) to isolate the effect of explicit polynomial features from the training-budget confound.
  • Train gamma and kinetic energy on log-transformed targets to test the log-uniform-sampling hypothesis above.
  • Drop the redundant beta (or velocity) target and re-measure whether the freed model capacity improves the remaining 7 targets.
  • Generate a genuinely held-out extrapolation set for Iteration 2 (e.g. β restricted to (0.999999, 1) or masses/forces outside the current log-uniform ranges) to test true out-of-distribution generalization under the widened training distribution.
  • Extend the scikit-learn sweep's normal/extreme comparison (currently run only for the two neural networks) to the tree-based models for a complete like-for-like comparison.

Synthesis

Standard regression models — from linear regression to gradient-boosted trees to small MLPs — can recover several relativistic quantities from this simulator's synthetic data to R² > 0.95 (position, velocity, beta, gamma, proper time with the best model), provided the training distribution covers the range being evaluated.

The project's clearest, best-supported finding concerns experimental design rather than any single model: the composition of the training distribution affects accuracy more than model choice. Widening the training β range between Iteration 1 and Iteration 2 converted a catastrophic extrapolation failure (R² as low as −1.7×10⁹) into strong in-distribution performance across the same target set — a larger effect than any difference observed between model families within either iteration.

Momentum, kinetic energy, and total energy remain comparatively difficult even in-distribution, and gamma shows the widest spread across model families. These are treated here as open problems for future iterations rather than settled conclusions.
